[17] Cod. X cites: Ἀ. εἰς νακτά. Ο΄. εἰς ἀσάλευτον. The same at Deut. vi. 8: Ἀ. HAKTA (sic). Ο΄. ἀσάλευτα. “The reading of Aquila is variously given in the MSS. The Roman edition has: Ἀ. καὶ εἰς ἐνεκτὰ, which certainly means nothing [Schleusner in _Opusc. Crit._ p. 101: ‘I think it means _things carried_, from ἐνεγκεῖν, _to carry, to bear_’]. The Basle codex here: καὶ εἰσινακτὰ, from which you will fish out no more information. The same Basle codex below, at Deut. vi. 8, on the word טֹטָפֹת has: Ἀ. ἀνακτὰ, which likewise means nothing. I think all these readings are corrupt [including the Coislinian, εἰς νακτὰ, which became known to him later]; and I am of opinion that we should read καὶ εἰς ἀτίνακτα, _into things unmoved_, so that it may agree with the other translators, καὶ ἀσάλευτον.”—_Montef._ I think εἰς νακτὰ is genuine, from νάσσω, _I stuff, I fill densely._ The Glossae give: Νακτὴ, _farsa_ [stuffed]. Νακτὸν, τὸ πεπιλωμένον, _dense, pressed._ That is, by this name the translator calls the cases into which the parchment slips, inscribed with various small sections of the law, _were stuffed._ The etymology of the Hebrew word, about which lexicographers dispute to this day, our translator seems to derive from the Talmudic טְפַף, _to make level, to smooth_, properly of measures accurately full, πεπιεσμένοις καὶ σεσαλευμένοις (Luke vi. 38), which agrees excellently with the Greek νάσσειν (in Hesychius ὁμαλίζειν, θλίβειν).
